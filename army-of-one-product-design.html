01 / THE PITCH

Executive product vision

Army of One is a tactical action shooter about accumulating an army from your own past lives. The player controls exactly one soldier at a time. On death, they choose to begin as a new soldier at an available insertion point or resume a soldier whose death was prevented in a prior run. Earlier soldiers replay their actions in subsequent attempts, changing the battlefield with every life. Players can win through mechanical skill, persistence, coordinated timing, geographic positioning, and timeline strategy—without commanding units like an RTS.
Fantasy

Be your own army

Every soldier fighting beside you was once directly controlled by a player.

Differentiator

Rewrite outcomes

A soldier marked for death can be rescued, producing a continuation point at the precise moment their recording runs out.

Accessibility

Failure creates progress

Repeated deaths can build greater strength. Optional auto-targeting emphasizes strategy and positioning rather than aim skill.

Audience and feel

Designed for players who enjoy emergent tactics, top-down shooters, time-manipulation puzzles, and collaborative experimentation. The tone can support large-scale military scenarios without demanding military simulation realism. The defining feeling should be: “I planned that rescue three lives ago, and it finally worked.”

Product principles

  1. One active soldier per life. Never allow mid-life character swapping.
  2. The timeline is the strategy interface. Respawns are decisions, not merely resets.
  3. Actions, not commands. The player creates tactics by playing soldiers, not issuing orders to units.
  4. Respect history without worshipping pixels. Record important outcomes precisely; simplify inconsequential movement when safe.
  5. Many routes to success. Skill, aggression, persistence, rescues, flank routes, and forward deployment all deserve viable playstyles.
  6. Readable complexity. The player must understand why an echo stopped, a soldier was rescued, or an entry point became available.

03 / HEART OF THE GAME

Core life-and-respawn loop

  1. Choose a deployment. Select an available insertion point and loadout (where the mission allows choices).
  2. Play exactly one soldier. Move, fight, interact, capture, take cover, or rush an objective. Replay soldiers act alongside you.
  3. Reach an endpoint. The life ends at death, victory, or mission completion. There is no switching away during the life.
  4. Review the timeline. Observe every soldier's lane, death X markers, open O markers, interruptions, and relevant mission events.
  5. Choose the next move. Start a new soldier from an available location or select an eligible O to continue a rescued soldier.
  6. Change the outcome. New actions can save earlier soldiers, unlock new insertion points, change combat outcomes, or disrupt previously recorded lives.
  7. Repeat until mission success. The composition and geographic reach of your self-created army grow through play.
Non-negotiable: An O is only selectable from a respawn screen. Soldiers cannot be swapped during active play. Continuing an O extends the same soldier's timeline rather than creating a new soldier.
04 / THE SIGNATURE UI

Timeline selection, visually

The respawn screen is both a history viewer and the primary strategic decision screen. Each soldier is represented by one horizontal lane; duration corresponds to battlefield time, not real-world wall-clock duration spent across retries.

Interactive sequence: select a stage to see a rescue create a selectable continuation.

What each event should communicate

X / Fatal

Recorded death

A life terminates. The next player may start a new soldier and attempt to prevent that death.

O / Resumable

Death averted

An originally doomed soldier is alive when their recording ends. This is an eligible continuation choice at a subsequent respawn.

Static / waiting

Recording exhausted

In the world, a saved soldier becomes a flickering noninteractive figure at the O if not chosen to continue.

! / Disrupted

Playback interruption

An echo is unable to follow its prior actions; show where it stopped while retaining the lane's prior duration for comparison.

Continuation rules

If Soldier 1 dies, Soldier 2 can replay alongside Soldier 1 and rescue them. At the next respawn Soldier 1's lane now ends in an O, while Soldier 2's lane may end in an X. Selecting Soldier 1's O resumes direct control from that precise time and state. Choosing a new soldier instead leaves Soldier 1 as a replaying echo until their O, where they freeze with a static/time-distortion effect. They do not autonomously invent new behavior beyond the recording.

A recorded soldier whose route is obstructed may attempt to adapt and regain their path. If recovery is not possible or plausible, it freezes at the interruption location. Its timeline marks the interruption in addition to showing its former extent. This can cascade into other rescues, deaths, and objective outcomes. These consequences are intentional strategic texture, but the UI must make them legible.

05 / PLAYER EXPERIENCE

Combat, controls, and accessibility

Moment-to-moment play

The first demo should feel like a responsive top-down tactical shooter: movement, aiming, firing, enemies, cover, and mission objectives. Combat readability matters more than elaborate physics or simulated ballistics. Hitscan weapons are sufficient; effects can visually suggest tracers and impact without requiring elaborate projectile mechanics.

Manual / precision

Twin-stick or mouse aim

The player moves and independently aims/fires. Enables deliberate precision shooting, suppressive lanes, and skilled stealth tactics.

Optional / casual

Auto-targeting shooter

The player primarily controls movement and positioning. The soldier selects and engages viable enemies automatically. Useful for phone play and players who prefer tactics to dexterity.

Auto-targeting is not intended to remove tactical choice: cover, timing, approach angle, spacing, and presence at important locations still matter. The product should clearly communicate targeting range and behavior. Exact target-selection priorities are an open balance decision.

How auto-targeting affects echoes

Manual shots should replay with meaningful shot timing and direction. Automatic shooting could either reproduce recorded shot decisions or retarget currently valid enemies. The latter may make echoes more helpful and the evolving battlefield more dynamic, but must be evaluated against predictability, causal correctness, and clarity. Treat this as a design experiment, not a hard requirement.

Input expectations

Death should not feel like punishment

Keep death-to-respawn flow short, while preserving the moment of strategic choice on the timeline. Highlight opportunities to rescue earlier soldiers and show the player's expanding army. The game can celebrate clever sacrificial runs without making repeated self-elimination a tedious optimal chore.

06 / HISTORY WITHOUT BLOAT

Record what matters. Simplify what doesn't.

Proposed optimization philosophy: Preserve significant actions and outcomes; lazily remove unnecessary movement points. Test whether the simplified echo still reaches an acceptably equivalent outcome. If a removed point causes an unacceptable divergence (for example, the soldier dies early), reject that removal. Aggressiveness can increase for old, low-impact portions of the battlefield as the army grows.

What must retain meaning

Important moments include shot timing and direction, movement state at a significant shot, damage, death, rescues, interactions, and capture of objectives or insertion points. The precise movement between such moments can often be interpolated from fewer timestamps and positions. Timing matters: a simplified path must not casually shift a soldier's arrival out of alignment with a necessary event.

Conceptual only: real simplification must be event-aware and validated in context. The straight line is appropriate only when interaction consequences remain acceptable.

Lazy, outcome-tested optimization

  1. Wait until worthwhile. Avoid optimizing tiny histories or sacrificing development simplicity too soon.
  2. Select candidate nodes. Prefer dense, redundant, old, or low-risk portions of recordings, especially early in a heavily populated mission.
  3. Temporarily remove points. Interpolate over the gap while preserving significant events and associated times.
  4. Validate the result. Re-evaluate the simplified replay in a representative context. If the soldier now dies earlier or breaks an important event, restore the points.
  5. Keep acceptable simplifications. Permit slight cosmetic differences—and possibly some harmless historical drift—where the player will not meaningfully notice.
  6. Repeat sparingly. Gradually improve density as recordings accumulate and performance demands grow.

A result being “the same” should mean materially equivalent for play, not mathematically identical movement. However, preserving one soldier's survival alone may be insufficient if their altered path disrupts someone else. The acceptance test should ultimately consider significant downstream outcomes, not only that individual soldier's death time.

Timey-wimey design license: Imperfect replay fidelity can be part of the fiction. Histories subtly shift as the battle evolves. But obvious inexplicable deaths, shots from the wrong location, or surprise invalidation of an earned O still feel like bugs. Fiction makes small deviations acceptable; it should not excuse unreadable rules.

Performance goals, expressed as product outcomes

The aspiration is to show large crowds of prior soldiers, potentially 100 or more, while maintaining responsiveness on typical desktop browsers. Mobile should degrade gracefully. Exact limits and performance budgets should be determined through benchmarking, not promised by the design document. Distinguish recording storage, simulation work, collision/combat, and drawing: shrinking files alone does not make every active soldier free.

07 / BATTLEFIELD GEOGRAPHY

Insertion points, captures, and the frontline

A mission may offer multiple initial approaches: landing by boat, paradropping, arriving by road, or entering from a flank. Additional deployment options can unlock when players capture tactical locations. New soldiers are not required to start at the earliest battlefield timestamp or original entry position.

Illustrative mission concept, not a committed map. B = boat, P = paradrop, R = road, ★ = forward capture point, ! = main objective.

Strategic consequences

Rush

Take ground first

Send an early soldier forward to capture a crossroads or new beachhead; future soldiers begin much closer to the fight.

Concentrate

Build a frontline

Spawn repeated reinforcements near the captured position instead of replaying a long opening march.

Diversify

Attack from several sides

Use distinct deployment routes so recorded soldiers create overlapping fields of fire and distractions.

Capture and availability rule — design decision needed

The current vision assumes that captures unlock future insertion choices, but it does not yet fix whether those unlocks are permanent for the match or conditional on the specific historical snapshot. A causal rule is thematic—if a crossroads capture was prevented, later spawning there may no longer be possible—but permanent unlocks may feel less punitive, particularly in casual mode. Both should be prototyped or consciously selected before mission authoring.

Likewise, forward insertion should identify both where and when a new soldier enters. A captured point at battlefield time 45 seconds might support insertion at or after that time rather than starting every soldier at time zero. The exact insertion-time rule is open and materially affects balance.

08 / A NEW KIND OF MULTIPLAYER

Shared history, independent attempts

The central rule: Players who are actively in the same synchronized instance see and affect one another immediately. When one dies and respawns separately, they begin a different instance using the recorded history available at the moment of respawn. They do not continuously receive subsequent actions from the other player's ongoing instance. Subsequent respawns can incorporate new history.
The diagram shows snapshot boundaries, not continuously shared alternative worlds. Two independent attempts may reach the same battlefield timestamp without becoming synchronized.

The careful player and the meat grinder

Player A · Precision

One life, deliberate advance

A takes careful shots, uses cover, and spends a long real-world time progressing through their first soldier's life.

Player B · Volume

Six soldiers in the same period

B rushes with B1, dies, zigzags with B2, dies again, and repeats. By the time A dies once, B has five completed lives and is actively playing B6.

When A finally respawns, A sees B1–B5 replaying, plus the portion of B6 already recorded at that snapshot moment. The snapshot of B6 is not continuously extended as B keeps playing elsewhere. It may end in a noninteractive incomplete-recording state. A might save some of B's doomed soldiers, and those opportunities can become visible to B on a later respawn while A's attempt is still ongoing.

Same-time play does not automatically imply live synchronization

Two players can independently replay battlefield time 45 seconds while occupying different instances. They do not automatically see each other's live actions. Only genuine shared-instance play does that. A proposed convenience is to delay a spawn at an O briefly when another live player is near that battlefield moment, allowing the two players to rejoin a synchronized session when feasible. This should be optional or carefully bounded to avoid frustrating waits.

Co-op: different skill levels become complementary

The novice may create many echoes that absorb fire and occupy space; the expert may create a small number of precise, decisive soldiers. A successful cooperative mission can include both without requiring equal mechanical skill or equal lives used. A player who can rescue another player's echoes adds a further collaborative dimension.

Competitive: speed versus accumulated force

Players can attempt an early stealthy objective capture, deliberately manufacture a crowd of meat shields, seed preemptive firing lanes for future arrivals, or seize forward spawn points before building reinforcements. A player might shoot toward a route where they expect the opponent to appear later; the recorded shots create a future hazard without RTS commands.

Beyond two players

The conceptual model can extend to more than two players because each independent attempt consumes a set of prior recordings. Real-time session merging, conflict resolution, history/version visibility, and total simulation workload become increasingly important. Support for larger player counts is an aspiration requiring validation, not a committed demo requirement.

09 / EMERGENT TACTICS

Strategies players invent themselves

01

One-life hero

Try to complete the mission immediately with stealth, cover, and accuracy.

02

Human-wave assault

Rapidly build many soldiers to soak up enemy attention and create overwhelming presence.

03

Forward-base rush

Use an early life to capture a new insertion point, then bring subsequent lives into the battle much later and closer.

04

Rescue chain

Keep saving previously doomed soldiers, extending a few lives into an unusually capable coordinated squad.

05

Pre-fire trap

Record shots along a route in anticipation of a future enemy or player crossing it.

06

Multi-entry pincer

Build echoes from beach, road, and air approaches so future lives inherit a staged offensive.

07

Temporal sabotage

Block a known echo path to interrupt its role and potentially collapse the opposing plan.

08

Decoy corridor

Place prior soldiers in exposed positions to draw fire while a later soldier advances on another route.

09

Low-effort mobile support

Use auto-targeting to contribute movement patterns, bodies, and area coverage without precision aiming.

These are intended examples of strategies the rules may permit, not fixed classes, special abilities, or guaranteed optimal exploits. The game should reward discovery without forcing one dominant way to play.

10 / CONTENT & PROGRESSION

Mission design and progression

Mission ingredients

Maps should be constructed around meaningful temporal and spatial choices: multiple approaches, chokepoints, high-risk/high-reward forward insertion sites, defensible lanes, and objectives that become achievable through combined historical actions. Beach assaults, urban crossroads, outposts, convoy ambushes, and fortified compounds are natural examples.

Mission typeCore objectiveTimeline opportunity
Beachhead assaultEstablish a foothold then destroy a defended objective.Capture inland entry; replay waves from beach and forward road.
Crossroads seizureHold or capture a strategically valuable junction.Early rushing unlocks convenient deployment for later soldiers.
Fortified infiltrationReach and disable a defended installation.Choose precision, diversionary echoes, or a mass assault.
Multi-front objectiveCoordinate actions at separated targets.Use past lives to align tasks across battlefield timestamps.
Versus objective raceTake or destroy a contested objective before an opponent.Balance swift completion against building an army.

Weapons, roles, and vehicles

Potential later variety includes riflemen, snipers, heavy weapons, grenadiers, medics, engineers, scouts, protective equipment, and vehicles. Different roles could create distinct temporal contributions—long-range precision, area denial, cover, or capture utility. These are extensions, not prerequisites for demonstrating the core product. Avoid commitments to large inventories, complex ballistics, or logistics until the timeline is fun.

Progression possibilities

A mission-select structure, unlockable gear, optional performance awards, and increasingly intricate scenarios could support a full release. Possible performance dimensions include fewest lives, fastest battlefield-time victory, highest number of rescues, least collateral disruption, or clever use of insertion points. Progression should not undermine the central concept that extra lives can function as additional reinforcements.

11 / HUMAN READABILITY

UX, feedback, and identity

On the battlefield

  • Visually distinguish the live controlled soldier from echoes and frozen/static figures.
  • Make incoming fire and important hits legible even among many soldiers.
  • Give each newly captured insertion point a clear world-space and HUD notification.
  • Use brief distortions, frozen frames, or static for a recording that has run out or been interrupted.
  • When an echo adapts around an obstacle, communicate that it is diverging from its past without overwhelming the screen.
  • Consider subtle per-soldier identifiers so important echoes can be recognized on the timeline and battlefield.

On the respawn screen

  • Show soldiers as rows, aligned by battlefield time, with recognizable X and O endpoints.
  • Provide hover/tap details for deaths, rescues, captures, significant shots, and interruptions.
  • Make valid continuation and spawn selections unambiguous; never visually imply an inactive frozen figure can be controlled mid-life.
  • Let players see where a soldier was interrupted relative to their originally recorded lane length.
  • Show entry locations geographically and their corresponding insertion timestamps.
  • In multiplayer, make it clear which recordings and partial histories were available in this respawn snapshot.
  • Keep the respawn decision quick despite a potentially dense, zoomable timeline.

Tone and fiction

Time alteration can justify old recordings becoming slightly different, especially after repeated timeline changes. Terms like “temporal echoes,” “unstable history,” and “static remnants” are promising but optional. The specific military setting, fictional technology, and narrative explanation are not yet locked. Presentation should sell the fantasy without requiring the player to study time-travel lore.

12 / ENGINEERING HANDOFF

Playable web demo: the product slice

The first demo is a 2D browser game intended to answer one question: Is it fun and understandable to die, replay prior soldiers, save one, and then continue them from an O? It should also establish whether accumulated echoes can be displayed responsively. A Phaser 4 + TypeScript frontend is a strong candidate because of existing familiarity, straightforward browser delivery, and AI-assisted development. The exact internal engineering architecture is for the implementation team to decide.

Must demonstrate

  • A responsive top-down player soldier with movement, basic hitscan weapon, enemies, health, and death.
  • A recorded prior life replays meaningfully while a newly spawned soldier acts alongside it.
  • A second soldier can prevent the first soldier's X, causing an O to become available.
  • The respawn UI shows distinct soldier lanes and enables selection of a new start or valid O.
  • Selecting O resumes the same soldier at the relevant battlefield moment, extending the lane until death or completion.
  • Choosing a new soldier instead leaves an uncontinued rescued echo frozen/static at its O.
  • A simple obstacle interruption produces an understandable frozen state and timeline marker.
  • At least two entry locations or one capturable forward deployment option demonstrate geographic choice.
  • Basic observability or stress-testing proves that many echoes can be exercised without manually playing dozens of lives.

Strong follow-ups, not blockers

  • Optional auto-aim mode with a clear manual/automatic control switch.
  • Lazy, event-aware recording-point removal with rollback when important outcomes change.
  • More than one enemy type, one mission variant, and more sophisticated map topology.
  • A functional multiplayer experiment demonstrating independent respawn snapshots.
  • Touch controls and a mobile performance pass.
  • Improved animation and 3D feasibility exploration.

Explicitly out of scope for the first slice

Full campaign, character progression economy, vehicles, advanced ballistics, realistic military physics, large server infrastructure, ranked multiplayer, polished 3D assets, and dozens of mission classes. These could all be worthwhile later, but none is needed to prove the unique game loop.

13 / PHASED PRODUCT DELIVERY

Recommended sequence

Phase 1 · Prove the mechanic

Single-player time echoes

One map, straightforward combat, recordings, respawns, X/O outcomes, continuation, and static echoes. The emotional payoff is seeing your own actions save yourself.

Phase 2 · Give it depth

Entry points and mission decisions

Forward captures, multiple approaches, more meaningful enemy layouts, interface improvements, and gameplay balance between speed and army building.

Phase 3 · Scale the army

Optimization and controls

Outcome-tested lazy simplification, hundreds-of-echo stress scenes, auto-targeting, touch-friendly UX, and gameplay feedback based on testing.

Phase 4 · Prove multiplayer

Snapshot-based co-op / versus

Independent respawns, partial histories, selective synchronized play, and rules for propagating rescues and mission outcomes.

Phase 5 · Expand the product

More mission variety

Roles, loadouts, larger maps, objectives, vehicles if justified, achievements, and long-term replayability.

Future option · 3D

New visual perspective

Explore 3D rendering without discarding the product's timeline and event concepts. Evaluate camera readability and performance as carefully as technical portability.

Success signals for a demo

Look for players who deliberately plan a rescue, can articulate the distinction between X and O, willingly experiment with alternate insertion points, and report the sensation of coordinating with themselves. A strong test is whether someone, without being told, notices a prior soldier's death and decides to intervene in the next attempt.

Qualitative fun and comprehension should dominate early decisions. Raw echo-count benchmarks are useful engineering evidence, not the core product success metric.

16 / HANDOFF NOTE

Instructions for the engineering partner

Protect the product invariant: A player remains in direct control of one soldier until that life ends; only at respawn can they select a different soldier's legitimately resumable O or create a new soldier from an eligible insertion point. This is not a free-possession mechanic and not a globally synchronized round-based game.

Build for a playable browser demonstration rather than a full game. Keep the experience legible with placeholder art if necessary. Phaser 4 and TypeScript are recommended, not a mandate to over-engineer. Basic hitscan combat and simplified physics are adequate. Ask product questions only where the decision changes player-facing behavior, especially O eligibility, interrupted echoes, forward insertion timing, and cross-instance multiplayer history rules.

Any implementation optimization should be evaluated by its impact on visible outcomes. The desired recording strategy is flexible: preserve meaningful events, lazily simplify low-impact movement, compare outcomes, and revert changes that cause unacceptable divergence. Do not mistake 100 recorded paths for a requirement to simulate 100 full human-controlled agents.

The initial milestone is not a visual showcase. It is the moment the player sees a soldier they previously watched die now survive, become an O on the timeline, and later continue under direct control.
